What is held back

When a state joins a human rights treaty, it may say, in the same act, which parts it does not accept, or how it will read them: a reservation, or a declaration. It may later withdraw what it held back. The United Nations keeps the record of these statements. Scholars have coded it provision by provision, for every state that joined nine of the treaties, up to {{LAST}}.

Read that way, no treaty is held whole by everyone who joined it, and most of what was held back is still held back: {{WD}} of {{HELD}} held-back provisions ({{WDP}}) have been withdrawn, after a median of {{MED}} years.

Where it gathers

In the file, the provision held back by the most states is article 16 (1) of the Convention on Discrimination against Women, on equality in marriage and family relations: {{C161}} of its {{CEDAWP}} parties. But the shape is not only a few dark columns. In the Convention against Torture only {{CATANY}} of {{CATP}} parties hold anything back, yet {{CATALL}} of its {{CATN}} coded provisions is held by all: a handful of statements, some of them covering a whole run of articles at once, are enough to leave almost no provision whole.

What stood still in {{LAST}} had stood for a median of {{STILLMED}} years; what was withdrawn had stood {{MED}} years (half of the withdrawals between {{Q1}} and {{Q3}}).

How far the coding can be trusted

Forty rows of the file were drawn at random and read again, by this practice, against the United Nations' own pages, without seeing how the file codes them. Where the pages could be read, the reading and the file agree at {{AGREE}} of {{TOLD}}. Four of the disagreements are the file being finer than the reading (it codes paragraphs, the reading counted articles), and one is a footnote the reading missed. The pages have moved on since {{LAST}}: they record withdrawals as late as 2025 that the file cannot hold.

After {{LAST}}: what came back, and what arrived

The file stops in {{LAST}}; the United Nations' pages do not. For each state that still held something back from a treaty in {{LAST}} ({{STAND}} such pairs could be found on the pages), the pages were searched for a withdrawal dated 2015 or later. And each state that joined one of the nine treaties after {{LAST}} was read for what it held back on joining.

In twelve years, {{RET}} of the {{STAND}} ({{RETP}}) withdrew something, of the ten read by hand seven only in part. In the same years, {{ARR}} times, a state joined a treaty holding something back.

The clauses the file leaves out

The file codes only the provisions that oblige a state to do or refrain from something. Five of the nine treaties also say how a dispute between states over the treaty is settled, by arbitration or by the International Court of Justice, and let a state refuse that clause when it joins; the Convention against Torture also lets it refuse the Committee's power to inquire. These refusals were read from the United Nations' pages as they stand today, every state's own statement, by a rule that counts an article as held back when a state's statement names it and no note records it withdrawn.

In every one of the five treaties, the article held back by the most parties is the one that sends disputes to a court. On women's rights, {{C29}} parties refuse article 29; article 16, on marriage and family, is held back by {{C16}} by the same rule.

A bar's full width would be fifty parties. Across the five treaties, {{CLNOW}} refusals of a court stand by that rule. The rule was checked by reading 24 statements by hand without seeing its answer; the two agreed at {{AGREE3}} of 24. Both misses are the rule counting too many: one state withdrew its refusal in December 2025 inside its own statement, and one names the clause to accept the Court. So the first section's sentence was a sentence about the file: what states hold back most is not an obligation but the way to be held to one.

What this page does not do. It does not judge any reservation lawful or unlawful, and it ranks no state. A provision held back is a fact about a statement made in the open, which the state may withdraw; the people the treaties protect are those it touches, and the page says no more about them than the record does.
